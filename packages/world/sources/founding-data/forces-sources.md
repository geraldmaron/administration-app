# W5 forces research: sources and caveats

Written 2026-09-24 for W5/5C (`world seed/{opening-world.ts,us-basing.tsv}`, `munitions.ts`, `basing.ts`) and its SEC-16 and SEC-25 acceptance criteria (register `README.md:382`, `:390`). Every source below was opened on 2026-09-24 unless marked otherwise. This note does not edit `waves/w3`, `waves/wl`, `waves/w4`, `README.md` or `HANDOFF.md`; it only documents the two data files beside it.

## Scope and honest limits

- `us-basing.tsv` covers 32 countries or territories in 46 rows: 38 installation rows and 8 country totals (count corrected in the revision pass, 2026-09-24). The CRS overseas-basing report (opened below, but its PDF body did not render to readable text) states DoD "manages or uses at least 128 overseas bases in at least 51 different countries." This file is a **sourced sample of the major, named installations**, not the full 128-base/51-country inventory. Treat any country not listed as unresearched, not as base-free.
- `munitions.tsv` has 12 US rows: ten munition systems (THAAD, Patriot, Tomahawk, JASSM, PrSM/ATACMS, SM-3, SM-6, 155mm shells, and the 2023-vintage Javelin and Stinger), the AN/TPY-2 radar and the HIMARS launcher, which are enablers, not munitions (count corrected in the revision pass). The Javelin and Stinger rows are very likely stale after the 2025-2026 war. For every other major military (Russia, China, North Korea, Iran, NATO Europe, other allies) no public stock figure exists; those rows carry a historian-constructed ordinal index (1 = minimal/import-dependent, 5 = highest observed), not an official index, built only from the comparative figures cited in the same row.
- Two figure types are mixed in `us-basing.tsv` and flagged per-row: (a) country-level active-duty totals aggregated from DMDC data (via a Wikipedia page that cites DMDC as of 2025-06-30; I could not reach a live DMDC 309A file directly, see below), and (b) base-level "community" figures from press coverage that usually include dependents, DoD civilians and contractors, not just active-duty troops. These two figure types are not directly comparable; do not sum them.
- The CSIS "Last Rounds?" report (April 2026) is itself reporting on a real, very recent Iran war (the "Twelve-Day War") whose own start date the report states inconsistently (one figure references "2025," most of the report references "2026"). This note does not resolve that; it is flagged as a source-level inconsistency, not corrected by inference.

## Sources opened, in the order used

1. **CSIS, "Last Rounds? Status of Key Munitions at the Iran War Ceasefire," April 2026.** Opened 2026-09-24 via `https://www.csis.org/analysis/last-rounds-status-key-munitions-iran-war-ceasefire` (WebFetch rendered the page text; the underlying PDF at the S3 URL failed to decode). Carries: pre-war and post-ceasefire stock figures for THAAD, Patriot (PAC-2/PAC-3), AN/TPY-2 radars, Tomahawk, JASSM, PrSM/ATACMS, SM-3 and SM-6, plus stated production rates and target increases. This is the single richest source in the file and the primary basis for every US interceptor/missile row.
2. **CSIS, "Empty Bins in a Wartime Environment: The Challenge to the U.S. Defense Industrial Base," January 2023.** Attempted open via the CSIS S3 PDF of 2023-01-19 (file name withheld: it carries the author's name) (WebFetch 2026-09-24); the PDF's binary/encoded stream did not decode to clean text on the first attempt, but a structured extraction of Javelin and Stinger figures (transfer totals, production rates, lead times, and CSIS's own rebuild-time model) was recovered and used. Treat the Javelin/Stinger rows as **thin**: the extraction could not be cross-checked against a second read of the same PDF, and the figures are 2022-2023 vintage, predating the 2025-2026 war that likely drew both systems down further.
3. **Military Times, "Manufacturing woes hamper US 155-mm ammo production," 2026-07-14.** Opened 2026-09-24 via `https://www.militarytimes.com/news/your-military/2026/07/14/manufacturing-woes-hamper-us-155-mm-ammo-production/`. Carries the current 155mm production rate (about 36,000 rounds/month as of March 2026), the missed 100,000/month-by-October-2025 goal, an Army inspector-general finding on the Mesquite, TX bottleneck, and a December 2027 interim target of about 140,000/month.
4. **National Defense Magazine, "Army Falls Short of 155mm Production Goal," 2025-08-14.** Not independently opened; the WebFetch attempt on `https://www.nationaldefensemagazine.org/articles/2025/8/14/army-falls-short-of-155mm-production-goal` returned no extractable content. Cited in `munitions.tsv` only as a corroborating title, not as a source of any specific figure.
5. **Army FY2026 "Procurement of Ammunition, Army" budget justification book**, at `https://www.asafm.army.mil/Portals/72/Documents/BudgetMaterial/2026/Discretionary%20Budget/Procurement/Procurement%20of%20Ammunition.pdf`. Found via search, not independently opened by WebFetch (PDF rendering failed for this class of document throughout this session). A search-engine summary reported "ARTILLERY PROJECTILE, 155MM" quantities (FY2024 actual 1,987,520; FY2025 enacted 171,657; FY2026 request 218,877) that are internally inconsistent (a 91% year-over-year drop from FY2024 to FY2025 with no explanation found) and were **not used** in `munitions.tsv` for that reason. If a future session can render the PDF directly, re-derive this row from the primary document rather than trusting the search summary.
6. **Euromaidan Press, summarizing the Estonian Foreign Intelligence Service's 2026 "International Security and Estonia" report,** opened 2026-09-24 via `https://euromaidanpress.com/2026/02/11/russian-shell-production-reaches-7-million-annually-17-times-pre-invasion-levels-estonian-intelligence/`. Carries Russia's 2025 combined shell/mortar/rocket production estimate (over 7 million, broken out by category), the 2024 comparison figure (4.5 million), the "17 times pre-invasion" framing, and the combined Iran+North Korea shell-export estimate (5-7 million since 2023). This is a secondary summary of a national intelligence assessment, not an audited figure; the underlying Estonian FIS report (`raport.valisluureamet.ee`) was found in search results but not independently opened.
7. **National Defense Magazine and related trade press on European 155mm production** (search results only; no single article independently opened). Carries the range of figures used in the "NATO Europe" row of `munitions.tsv` (stated capacity 1.7-2 million/year vs. estimated actual output ~580,000; Rheinmetall's stated 700,000/year-by-2025 and 1.1-1.5 million/year-by-2027 targets). Flagged in the TSV note as secondary and unverified in detail; the figures across outlets do not agree with each other, which is itself reported.
8. **Federation of American Scientists, summary of the DoD 2024 "Military and Security Developments Involving the People's Republic of China" (China Military Power Report),** opened 2026-09-24 via `https://fas.org/publication/the-2024-dod-china-military-power-report/`. Carries China's ICBM/IRBM launcher and missile counts as of mid-2024 and DoD's own uncertainty caveat about what counts as "operational." Does **not** carry any conventional-munitions or air-defense-interceptor figure for China; none exists in the public record as far as this research went.
9. **CRS, "U.S. Overseas Basing: Background and Issues for Congress" (R48123), July 10, 2024.** Attempted open via `https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R48123/R48123.2.pdf` and via `https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R48123.html`, both on 2026-09-24. The EveryCRSReport HTML version rendered a usable excerpt: the "68 persistent bases and 60 other US military sites," the "128 overseas bases in at least 51 countries" figure, and the five combatant-command regions used to organize the report. It did **not** yield the report's own country-by-country base table or its discussion of which countries use which host-nation agreement type; that level of detail in `us-basing.tsv` comes from the country-specific searches below, not from this CRS report directly.
10. **Wikipedia, "United States military deployments,"** opened 2026-09-24, stating it draws on Defense Manpower Data Center figures as of 2025-06-30. This is the source for every country-level active-duty total in `us-basing.tsv` (Japan 53,912; South Korea 23,766; Germany 35,989; Italy 12,571; UK 10,071; Spain 3,700; Turkey 1,717; Bahrain 3,391; Kuwait 559; Qatar 254; Saudi Arabia 270; Egypt 188; Australia 315; Singapore 260; Diego Garcia 230; Guam 7,125; Cuba 571; Honduras 340; Puerto Rico 774). **This is a secondary source citing a primary one.** A direct DMDC/DoD 309A ("Active Duty Military Personnel Strengths by Regional Area and by Country") file was searched for repeatedly and not found at a stable, fetchable URL in this session; `dwp.dmdc.osd.mil` appeared in search results but its report pages were not reachable by WebFetch. A future session should try to pull the primary DMDC file directly rather than continue relying on the Wikipedia aggregation.
11. **Base-specific press and official pages**, each opened or searched individually and cited per-row in `us-basing.tsv`: Al Udeid (Wikipedia, GlobalSecurity, Responsible Statecraft), the air base at Al-Kharj (Wikipedia, GlobalSecurity, a war.gov release on access to an air base at Taif), Al Dhafra (state.gov 2017-2021 archive, The Week), the expeditionary base in Djibouti (South China Morning Post, Washington Post, Wikipedia), the Philippines EDCA sites (PACOM and war.gov official releases, US Embassy Manila fact sheet), Marine Rotational Force-Darwin (Australian Department of Defence pages, Nautilus Institute), Pituffik/Greenland (multiple September 2026 press: U.S. News, Stars and Stripes, CBS News, Defense News), NATO SOFA text (state.gov's hosted 1951 treaty text), Diego Garcia (Air & Space Forces Magazine on the 2026 UK-Mauritius-US access deal), Kwajalein/Compact of Free Association (Congress.gov CRS product IF12194, Wikipedia), Ramstein, Kadena and the Pyeongtaek garrison personnel figures (SlashGear, press aggregations), Rota/Aviano/Souda Bay/Incirlik (CEPA, defense press). None of these individually changes the overall picture; each is a single-fact citation for one row and is not re-listed here in full; see the `note` column of `us-basing.tsv` for the specific claim each supports.

## What is unverified or thin, named plainly

- **The Javelin and Stinger rows** (2023 CSIS figures) are the weakest in `munitions.tsv`: dated, extracted from a PDF that would not decode cleanly, and almost certainly superseded by the 2025-2026 war's consumption. Do not treat them as current stock.
- **The Iran row** in `munitions.tsv` has no figure-bearing source opened at all; it is inference from adjacent rows and general awareness that Iran was a belligerent in the war CSIS's "Last Rounds" report describes. It should be re-researched before anything in the game reads it as more than a placeholder.
- **The "Other NATO/allied militaries" row** is a single blended ordinal judgment covering many different countries (UK, France, Japan, South Korea, and others) with materially different industrial bases. It should not be read as applying uniformly; it is a floor assumption for countries the game needs a number for and this research did not reach individually.
- **The FY2026 Army ammunition budget-justification figures** (item 5 above) were found but explicitly **not used** because they looked internally inconsistent from a search summary alone; a primary re-read is needed before trusting a specific procurement quantity for 155mm shells beyond the production-rate figures already used from Military Times.
- **The exact start date of the "Twelve-Day War"** referenced throughout the CSIS "Last Rounds" report is inconsistent within the source itself (2025 in one place, 2026 in most others). This note repeats the source's own figures without resolving the inconsistency.
- **The DMDC country totals** are one step removed from the primary DoD report (via a Wikipedia aggregation dated 2025-06-30); no live link to the primary DMDC 309A report was found working during this session.
- **CRS R48123's own country-by-country base table and host-agreement-type discussion** did not render from either PDF attempt or the EveryCRSReport HTML excerpt actually opened; the agreement-type column in `us-basing.tsv` is built country-by-country from separate searches, not from a single authoritative CRS table.

## For whoever builds `basing.ts` / `munitions.ts` from these files

- The `figure_source` / provenance flags in both TSVs are there so the engine or seed-data reviewer can decide, per row, whether a figure is precise enough to seed a stat or only precise enough to rank countries against each other (the ordinal index in `munitions.tsv` is explicitly built for the latter use, not the former).
- Where a row says a figure was "not opened" or "not independently opened," treat it as **not sourced** for the doctrine's purposes even though it appears in the file; it is there so the next research pass knows what is still owed, not because it has been verified.

## Revision pass (2026-09-24)

- **Stock counts.** A second read of the CSIS "Last Rounds?" page (`https://www.csis.org/analysis/last-rounds-status-key-munitions-iran-war-ceasefire`, opened 2026-09-24, published 2026-04-21 and updated 2026-04-24) found no pre-war or post-ceasefire stock count for THAAD, Patriot, Tomahawk, JASSM, SM-3, SM-6 or PrSM in the page text. The page says "Munition inventories are classified, but publicly available information is sufficient to make estimates." Its one count is an estimate that perhaps 800 shorter-range ATACMS remain. The stock figures in `munitions.tsv`'s THAAD and Patriot rows (452, 234 to 278, 2,330, 827) may sit in a chart the fetch tool cannot read, as the challenge's two reads also found. They are not seeded as counts (`../briefs.md` 5C.2-AC15).
- **Lead time.** The same read found a general lead time: "Historically, that has been about 24 months, but as munitions orders have outstripped production capacity in recent years, lead times have stretched to 36 months or more," and a full replacement cycle of "about 52 months in all—over four years." This is a general figure, not a per-system one; 5C.2 uses it as the restock lead for every class and labels it so.
- **Names.** Installation names that honor real people were relabeled in `us-basing.tsv` by place or function (the program's no-real-names rule); the file name of the 2023 CSIS PDF is withheld for the same reason.



## Installation coordinates (2026-10-10)

Written 2026-10-10 for the map-first program's force placement. `us-basing.tsv` gained an anchor per row: `anchor` (`sourced` or `zone-level`), `lat`, `lon`, `anchor_source` and `anchor_opened`. Every coordinate below was read on 2026-10-10 from the Wikidata item's "coordinate location" (property P625), through the Wikidata API (`wbgetentities`) and cross-checked against the item's label and description so the item is the installation the row names. Where an item carries two coordinates, the preferred-rank one is used (Grafenwoehr); where both are normal rank, the first listed (Rota). Coordinates are rounded to four decimal places (about 11 meters).

**Why Wikidata rather than the Wikipedia article.** The English Wikipedia API exposed a primary coordinate for only nine of the 35 articles (Kadena, Camp Zama, Yokota, Ramstein, Lakenheath, Mildenhall, Changi, Pituffik, Guantanamo); the infobox coordinates on the rest are not tagged primary. The Wikidata item is the record those infoboxes draw from, and it is one checkable URL per row that never carries a person's name, which matters here: eleven installations are named for people (the Pyeongtaek garrison, the Vilseck barracks, the Stuttgart headquarters, the air bases near Constanta, west of Kuwait City and at Al-Kharj, the Djibouti base, the Darwin barracks, the Kwajalein airfield, the Kosovo camp and the Honduran air base), and the rule that relabeled those rows by place or function also keeps their names out of this file. The URL is the item number alone.

**Which item stands for a functional label.** "Stuttgart command headquarters" is anchored at the barracks in Vaihingen that hosts the European command's headquarters (Q2056645); the African command sits at a second barracks a few kilometers away. "Army garrison, Kwajalein Atoll" is anchored at the Army airfield on Kwajalein Island (Q3886454), because the atoll's own item points at the lagoon. "Marine rotational force, Darwin" is anchored at the Army barracks the rotation is hosted at (Q7352085). "Keflavik periodic rotations" is anchored at the former naval air station item (Q3481748), which is the airfield the rotations use. "Naval Support Activity Souda Bay, Crete" is anchored at the Greek naval base at Souda Bay that hosts it (Q23302630); Wikidata has no separate item for the US activity.

**Split rows.** Four composite rows became separate installations, each "in the country total": "Yokosuka, Camp Zama and Yokota" (Yokosuka naval base, Camp Zama, Yokota Air Base), "Grafenwoehr and Vilseck training area" (Grafenwoehr training area, Army barracks at Vilseck), "Lakenheath and Mildenhall air bases" (RAF Lakenheath, RAF Mildenhall), and "Camp Arifjan and the air base west of Kuwait City" (Camp Arifjan, Air base west of Kuwait City). The Kuwait row carried the country's DMDC band ("500 to 1,000 on the DMDC count, which undercounts rotations"); that band moved to a new Kuwait country-total row with the same source, so no per-base figure was invented. `BASING_SPLIT_FROM` (`dataset/us-basing.ts`) maps each old label to its parts for saves that still name it.

**Zone-level rows.** Five installation rows have no single location and are drawn in their host's zone, never at an invented site: Poland's rotational brigade and battle group, Lithuania's battalion rotation, Norway's Marine prepositioning and rotations, the nine agreed Philippine locations, and Egypt's exercise rotations. The nine country totals are zone-level by definition.

**Coverage.** 49 rows: 35 sourced anchors, 5 zone-level installations, 9 country totals.
