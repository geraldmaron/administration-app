# About the game

*A short, non-technical overview of The Administration. It is the plain-words version of `NORTH_STAR.md` §1 and `CONTENT_DOCTRINE.md` §0–§1; read those for the full statement. For how it is built see `ARCHITECTURE.md`, and for what the player sees see `DESIGN.md`.*

---

## The pitch

You are the President of the United States, taking office among the 196 governments a President deals with. Their statistics matter: GDP, military strength and regime stability are all read by the simulation, not painted on. You don't choose the seat or the country, and the charter is the Constitution.

The game is played on one living map of the world. When a matter needs you, the map turns to where it is happening: a state hit by a storm, a strait under threat, a capital that just changed hands. The decision arrives briefed: the memo opens on the stakes, the cabinet speaks in plain words and disagrees, a foreign government's words arrive only as a relayed document, and you choose a response and live with what happens next.

## What playing feels like

You read the price before you commit, and on the harder choices the odds, the single worst outcome, and who abroad will notice. You don't get exact numbers to optimize. What a decision cost in people, money and mood stays hidden until the Record shows it, and the map recolors the places it touched.

The world keeps moving whether or not you engage. Every other government acts on its own agenda, against you and against each other, and non-state operators (cartels, insurgencies, hacker crews) make problems with no diplomatic counterparty to call. Time runs until something needs you; a quiet fortnight passes quietly.

You can play the same presidency on a phone or a desktop. The layout changes; what you can do doesn't.

## The score

Approval is the headline: what the country thinks of you, built from six judgments the public makes. Beside it sit the treasury, stability, military readiness and standing with the world, plus a slow-moving alignment that tracks how your decisions read politically. Nothing wins. A presidency ends by removal, defeat at the polls, the end of a second term, or walking away.

## The player fantasy

You're not a god, a general or a founder. You're the person in the chair when the phone rings, with real formal power, real limits and no clean answers. The fantasy is competence under constraint: making hard calls with incomplete information and watching the consequences ripple through people who remember them.

## Tone

Plain words a layman reads once, in the register of a sharp newsroom rather than a policy paper. Grounded and journalistic, never cartoonish.
