# Political & Government Simulation Games: Field Census

## Scope

This census covers political and government simulation games across six major families: nation-leadership sims, narrative political games, campaign/election sims, city/governance management, grand strategy with deep political layers, and mobile-first political/text games. Coverage spans primarily PC/Steam games (the largest discoverability and review-count proxy), with iOS/Android coverage where data was available. Games included are those where governance, political decision-making, or executive-level authority is the primary mechanic, not a secondary layer. Excluded: pure 4X games (Civilization) where politics is incidental; role-playing games without governance focus (like Fallout); purely military strategy games without political systems.

## Mechanics Worth Stealing

### 1. Interest Groups & Constituency Modeling (Victoria 3 / Democracy 3 Model)

**What It Is:** Representation of distinct political constituencies (labor, capital, military, clergy, intelligentsia, etc.) with independent policy preferences and loyalty thresholds. Enacting laws pushes approval up/down per group; multiple groups with divergent values create genuine tension.

**Why It Works:** Moves away from a single "approval" meter. Players learn which groups to court for specific policies and experience realistic political coalition pressure. Victoria 3's interest groups actually block law passage if sufficiently opposed.

**Cost Given Our Systems:** We have Congress (House/Senate chambers + 7 leaders) and five approval drivers (treasury, stability, military readiness, international standing, calm). Adding granular interest groups would require:
- Refactoring approval logic to per-group basis
- New scenario authoring (which groups does this scenario affect?)
- UI complexity (showing group positions per law)
- 2–3 weeks of engine work + content integration

**Fit Score: 4/5** (High value; medium cost; aligns with our "who do we anger" question)

### 2. Multiple Endings Driven by Player Track Record (Suzerain / Crusader Kings Model)

**What It Is:** End-game state determined by cumulative player decisions, not a final vote. A player who neglected military and economy reaches a different ending than one who built them up, even if both stay in power. Endings branch on ideology, alliances formed, family welfare, scandals survived.

**Why It Works:** Replayability. Players see consequences ripple across the full arc. Moral coherence (did I become a dictator? reformer? survivor?). Removes binary win/lose and replaces with "which ending do I get?"

**Cost Given Our Systems:** We already track state (metrics, Congress alliances, removals triggered). Endings are expensive to author (prose, branching) but cheap to implement technically.
- Define 8–12 ending states (e.g., "Removed via impeachment," "Military coup," "Peacefully retired," "Returned to democracy," "Descended into tyranny")
- Checkpoint scenario conditions against those states
- Author prose for each ending
- Estimate: 3 weeks authoring + 1 week integration

**Fit Score: 5/5** (Core feature; aligns with our replay-from-seed model; high player value)

### 3. "Book of Laws" Dual-Choice Governance (Frostpunk Model)

**What It Is:** Policy decisions are mutually exclusive pairs (Order vs. Faith, Authoritarian vs. Democratic, Pragmatic vs. Moral). Picking one path locks the other; later laws build on earlier choices. Creates persistent governance shape.

**Why It Works:** Constrains player freedom in a meaningful way (you can't be both a tyrant and a reformer indefinitely). Early game choices gate late-game options. Ideology feels baked in, not retrofitted.

**Cost Given Our Systems:** We have executive orders and budget allocation but no branching governance ideology tree. Implementation:
- Define 3–4 dual-choice axis pairs (e.g., Surveillance vs. Privacy, Market vs. Regulation, Military vs. Diplomacy)
- Create policy trees that depend on prior choices
- Engine: track chosen ideology, gate scenario activation
- Estimate: 2 weeks design + 2 weeks implementation

**Fit Score: 4/5** (Strong fit; adds replayability and ideology flavor; medium cost)

### 4. Consequence Ripple System (Suzerain / Crisis in the Kremlin Model)

**What It Is:** A single decision cascades: answering a scenario affects multiple systems simultaneously (treasury, stability, relationships with named entities, congressional opinion). Consequences are authored, not procedurally rolled.

**Why It Works:** Players feel the weight of decisions. No "undo" — every choice commits you. Authored prose consequences feel more coherent than random stat changes.

**Cost Given Our Systems:** We have scenario/consequence/FeedItem infrastructure already. Expanding it:
- Current: one scenario → one consequence card → one or two stat shifts
- New: one scenario → multiple cards (press, Congress reaction, foreign response) across time
- Refactor consequence batch logic to support multi-week delays
- Estimate: 2 weeks refactoring + heavy content authoring

**Fit Score: 5/5** (Already partly baked in; high polish/depth; aligns with our prose-first model)

### 5. Cabinet/Advisory Council with Personality & Conflict (Yes Your Grace / Crusader Kings Model)

**What It Is:** Named advisers (generals, economists, diplomats, ideologues) with opinions, ambitions, and clashing advice. Player must balance their demands and can be undercut by them. Advisers can be fired or die in accidents.

**Why It Works:** Humanizes the simulation. Player develops attachments/rivalries with named characters. Adviser conflicts create natural story beats (should I replace my general?). Reduces the "reading a spreadsheet" feel.

**Cost Given Our Systems:** We have named Congress leaders but no Cabinet. Adding Cabinet:
- Define 6–8 Cabinet roles (Secretary of State, Defense, Treasury, etc.)
- Rig each to preference-track (hawk/dove, protectionist/free-trade, etc.)
- Author adviser dialogue for each role
- Engine: track adviser opinions, gate certain actions on their approval
- Estimate: 3 weeks design + 3 weeks authoring

**Fit Score: 4/5** (High charm; medium cost; works well in prose-first games; less mechanical depth than interest groups)

### 6. Escalation Ladder / Ratcheting Difficulty (Our Own Emerging Model)

**What It Is:** Not a novel mechanic but worth noting: our "removal pressure" ratchet and "grievance memory" (planned) create a tightening noose. Ignoring problems makes them worse; letting metrics tank triggers crises.

**Why It Works:** Prevents "I can just ignore this forever." Teaches consequences. Pressure escalation creates narrative intensity (things are falling apart).

**Cost Given Our Systems:** Already partially live (removal pressure). Completing it:
- Implement grievance memory (record what crises we ignored)
- Gate high-impact scenarios on unresolved grievances
- Tune escalation curve so players feel the squeeze at weeks 60–80
- Estimate: 2 weeks

**Fit Score: 5/5** (Ours; already planned)

## Their Weaknesses / Our Openings

### 1. Text-Heavy Narrative Games Lack System Depth

**Evidence:** Suzerain's 93% positive reviews praise the writing and moral dilemmas but reviews note "the simulation aspect is light" and "mostly a story with consequence tracking, not a deep strategy game." Players finish it in 3–5 hours and replay with different philosophies, but there's no emergent long-term system tension (economy mismanagement doesn't spiral).

**Our Opening:** We pair authored prose (narrative) with system depth (metrics, Congress, relationships that degrade over time). A bad economy doesn't just change a flag; it triggers cascading crises. Our longer arc (4-year term, 2 terms max) gives system emergent storytelling that one-off scenes cannot.

### 2. Grand Strategy Games Suffer from "Spreadsheet Syndrome"

**Evidence:** Victoria 3 reviews note "repetitive, boring micromanagement" and "the actual player experience is engaging your spreadsheet, not playing a game." Europa Universalis IV's diplomacy is praised but trade and economic layers are described as "busywork." The systems are deep but lack narrative glue.

**Our Opening:** Every action the player takes is authored as a memo/document to read. No bars to adjust; you answer documents. This shifts the texture from spreadsheet to desk. Documents are prose, carrying weight and personality that a dialog box cannot.

### 3. Governance Sims Rarely Model the Pressure of Personal Consequences

**Evidence:** Democracy 3 tracks "re-election risk" but it's a number; Frostpunk has population approval but no scandal system. No game tracks "your defense minister has been taking bribes and the press smells blood." This is where narrative games win (Suzerain tracks family pressure, your party doubts you). Simulation games don't.

**Our Opening:** Marry system pressure with named individuals who can betray, leak, or resign. A scandal scenario doesn't just tank approval; it triggers a specific person's exit. Our Congress leaders are named; make them have ambitions that conflict with our policies. Scenario consequences can say "Senator Chen breaks rank and calls for your removal" — that's far more visceral than "stability −5."

### 4. Most Games Offer No Risk of Removal (Win Condition Ambiguity)

**Evidence:** Tropico, Realpolitiks, Masters of the World, even Victoria 3: you can "lose" by being conquered or economically ruined, but removal via political process is rarely modeled (Frostpunk does it via "discontent reaches max"). Players never feel the guillotine's rope.

**Our Opening:** Impeachment / removal is real and achievable. A player playing badly is *removed from office*. They don't get to play indefinitely; they're fired. This is our single biggest differentiator and it creates genuine tension. No other head-of-state sim does this as the core removal path.

### 5. Mobile Political Games Are Simplistic (No System Depth)

**Evidence:** Reigns is brilliant at "make a choice and watch stats move" but there's no economy, no factional negotiation, no long-term arc. Head of State is 30 minutes of event responses. Politicus claims depth but is barely documented and reviews are scarce.

**Our Opening:** We're shooting for PC/web/mobile convergence (Expo player, one codebase). We can deliver Suzerain-level prose + Victoria 3-level system depth on mobile. Most games pick one or the other. We do both, ship mobile first, and the PC/web versions are the *same game*. That's unfilled space.

### 6. No Game Seriously Models Media/Press as a System

**Evidence:** Papers Please has media pressure as flavor. Beholder has informants. But no game makes "what does the press say about you?" a core strategic tool. Orwell flips it (you ARE the propaganda), but a player-as-president managing his own media narrative isn't modeled.

**Our Opening:** Our press/dispatch feed is authored, not procedural. Every consequence includes what the papers say about it. A scandal can be framed as "presidential decisiveness" or "executive overreach" depending on our story. Cabinet leaks can feed stories to the press. Make media a strategic battlefield where the player fights for narrative control.

### 7. Lack of True Asymmetry: AI Doesn't "Play Against You"

**Evidence:** Most sims have AI opponents but they follow the same rules. Terra Invicta's factions compete, but it's transparent. No game models "Congress is actively trying to remove you" or "the Joint Chiefs will stage a coup if military readiness drops below 30%." The opposition is passive until a threshold is crossed.

**Our Opening:** Model Congress as active opposition. They want to remove you. Senators have ambitions (push their pet bill or leak damaging info). The military wants its budget and influence. Make these actors pursue their goals even when the player succeeds elsewhere. A scandal becomes an *opportunity* for opponents to move against you. That's emergent political drama.

## Direct Competitors (★)

### ★ Suzerain (Torpor Games, 2020)

**Why It's a Direct Competitor:**
Suzerain is the closest analog. Player is a head of state (president of a fictional nation) navigating economic crisis, international tension, and domestic politics entirely through text-based decision trees. It's prose-first. Every decision ripples into policy, relationships, and family consequences. Multiple endings based on accumulated choices. It's explicitly marketed as a political simulation for ages 25+ with an adult, serious tone.

The gap: Suzerain is ~5 hours, one playthrough. Our game is a 4-year term (potentially 8 years), with a repeatable, deterministic, seeded simulation underneath. Suzerain is narrative-driven; we're system-driven with narrative overlay. But the core player experience (reading documents as a head of state, making hard choices) is the same.

**Evidence:** Suzerain has 93% positive reviews (7,166) on Steam, placing it in the top tier of indie games. The reviews consistently praise the "serious political simulation" angle and compare it favorably to The Political Machine, Victoria, and other sims. No other game has captured this positioning so successfully.

### ★ Papers, Please (Lucas Pope, 2013)

**Why It Could Be Considered a Competitor:**
Papers, Please is a document-reading game where the player processes bureaucratic decisions (admit/deny) under political pressure. It's authored prose (document face), not a spreadsheet. Every decision carries weight and moral consequence. It's reached 37,800+ reviews at 97% positive — the most acclaimed indie game ever. The texture (reading papers and making calls) is mechanically identical to ours.

**The gap:** Player is an immigration officer, not a head of state. No meta-system (no economy, no Congress, no leverage). Decisions are tactical (this person or that person) not strategic (policy direction). Playtime is 2–3 hours. But the *UX* — reading documents, making hard calls, seeing ripples — overlaps heavily.

**Why We're Not Direct Competitors Despite the UX Similarity:**
Papers, Please is mastered territory. We're going after *head of state* + *systems depth* + *replayability* in one game. Papers, Please is a masterpiece at "one night in a border" — a constrained scenario, not a term in office.

### Suzerain is the Real Competitor

Of the games surveyed, **Suzerain is the only direct competitor.** It occupies our positioning (text-driven, head-of-state, serious, adult, consequence-driven, multiple endings). It's well-reviewed and will be the player's reference point when evaluating us.

**How We Win:**
- **System Depth:** Suzerain has no underlying simulation; we do. Every decision interacts with economy, Congress, relationships, and cascades forward. Suzerain is 5 hours; we're 8–20+ hours per playthrough.
- **Replayability:** Suzerain has 9 endings, all based on the same scenario path. We have scenario variation (choose different bills, manage different crises) because our systems are live. Each playthrough unfolds differently.
- **Determinism & Seeding:** Suzerain's story tree is fixed (branching narrative). Our game is seeded; same seed = same game, different decisions = different outcome. This enables competitive play, speedrunning, sharing "runs."
- **Scale & Scope:** Suzerain is one fictional nation. We're the USA with 50 states, international relationships with 49 nations, elections, removal risks, wars. Scope and complexity are 10x.

## Game Families Not Accounted For

### 1. **Espionage/Intelligence Agency Sims**
Games like Spycraft, DEFCON, or Chimera Squad put the player in an intelligence or special operations role, not a head-of-state role. We don't cover this family, but it's distinct enough that its absence is worth noting. These are adjacent to political sims (agencies shape policy) but not political sims per se.

### 2. **Procedurally Generated / Roguelike Political Sims**
Most political games are authored (fixed scenarios, branching narrative). There's an unfilled niche for a roguelike political game where each run has procedurally generated crises, random Congress, random international events. We don't see games in this space. (Suzerain's scenario pool is fixed; Papers Please's cases are procedural but thematically identical.)

### 3. **Multiplayer Asynchronous Political Games**
All the games we surveyed are single-player or real-time multiplayer (Realpolitiks, Tropico). There's no asynchronous multiplayer political game (like a "play-by-email" grandmaster-level political sim). No game we surveyed does it.

### 4. **Mod/Creator Tools for Political Sims**
Democracy 3 has Steam Workshop. Europa Universalis has a thriving mod community. But no political game ships with *player-facing tools to author scenarios or rules*. A game where players can write their own political crises (like Twine for political sims) would be novel.

### 5. **Asymmetrical Political Games**
No game we found puts the player in an asymmetrical role (e.g., one player is the president, others are Congress; one is the press, others are the government). Statecraft: Nations tries this on Roblox (multiplayer RPG where players control different countries) but it's not a polished simulation.
