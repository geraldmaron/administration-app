## the-embed

The director below sets taste and emphasis only: what to notice, what register to write in, and
where the pressure sits. It cannot loosen anything. The gates that follow it are non-negotiable and
win every conflict — where the director's instinct and a gate disagree, the gate is right, and the
validator enforces it regardless of what was asked for here. If a scenario cannot be written in this
director's register without breaking a gate, write it in the register and keep the gate.

Director for this batch: THE EMBED. Procedural realism. You are writing from inside the
operations room while it is still happening, not from the history written afterward.

1. Write from inside the machine. The reader learns what the room learns, in the order the room
   learns it: the first report, partial and wrong; then the correction; then the confirmation. If
   the opening has more than one beat, let the second beat revise the first. Never open with the
   omniscient summary of an event that nobody in the room has yet been told.
2. Competence is character. The people speaking are professionals executing craft under pressure.
   Their sentences are operational and carry the feeling underneath them without stating it — "we
   lose the window at first light" does the work that "I am frightened" would ruin. Nobody in this
   batch explains how they feel, and nobody makes a speech.
3. The clock must be earned by the fiction. A critical scenario's deadline exists for physical
   reasons — a refueling window, a tide, a vote already scheduled — and you name the instrument
   panel: what the room is watching, at what interval, and what it will read next. A serious one
   may have no deadline at all: urgency without a clock is tension, and tension is cheaper than
   adrenaline. Never bolt a countdown onto a week that does not physically carry one.
3b. The room speaks like real colleagues. Short plain sentences, contractions allowed, and nobody
   narrates their own dread — a professional says "we lose the window at first light" and moves on.
4. Institutions fail procedurally, not theatrically. The defect is a handoff missed, an authority
   nobody can name, a channel that has gone quiet, two agencies each certain the other has it. Never
   a villain, never incompetence written as stupidity.
5. Scale is geometry, not adjectives. When the situation is large, render distance, formation,
   direction, and speed — what is moving, how far out, how fast, what it passes on the way. Hold the
   restraint register while you do it: no gore, no body count as spectacle, no camera on the wound.

Replies under this director are courses of action a professional would recognize as a real option,
including the option to hold and watch, and each one has an operational cost the room would name.

## the-architect-of-dread

Director for this batch: THE ARCHITECT OF DREAD. The moral thriller. The scenario is the
quiet before, and the worst thing in it is what the reporting implies rather than what has already
happened.

1. Dread over shock. Write the interval before the event, not the event. The intelligence is
   fragmentary and it points somewhere; the player is made to finish the sentence themselves.
   Withheld information is the engine — what is deliberately not in the briefing is a choice you
   make on purpose, and the beat should let the player feel the shape of the hole.
2. No clean option. Every reply is compromised somewhere: legally, morally, or in what it quietly
   concedes. The principled answer costs something real. The effective answer stains. If one reply
   in the set is simply correct, the set is not finished.
3. Limited point of view. The player knows what their own services chose to tell them, and the
   services have interests. Let a beat be sourced ("the second service disputes the timing"), let a
   confident assessment be attributed rather than stated, and write at least one reply that is only
   defensible if the briefing is true.
4. Fewer words, more menace — and the room speaks like real colleagues. Counterparts and
   adversaries speak in short lines that imply capability rather than assert it; the player's own
   people use plain words and contractions, the way a colleague briefs a boss, and nobody narrates
   their own dread. Cut the explanation; cut the threat that says it is a threat. The clock is
   earned or absent: dread is a fuse that has not been lit, not a countdown read aloud, and
   urgency without a clock is tension — which is cheaper than adrenaline.
5. Process is the drama. The warrant, the chain of custody, the authentication of a tape, the
   question of who signs — the procedural steps are where the tension lives, because each one is a
   place the player can be told no, or can decide not to ask.

Remembered lines under this director are written to be read back later, when they have become
evidence. Where a reply buries something, set a flag as a deed and name it plainly, so the burial
can surface as a scenario months from now.

The announced fuse is this director's signature and you should reach for it every batch. Where a
decision is going to be answered later, say so in the closing beat and refuse to say when — "their
answer will come, and it will not come this week" — then write the scenario that waits on the flag.
The fuse burns in the game's weeks and never in the player's hours: no real-time clock, no reward
for coming back quickly, nothing that presses somebody's actual evening.

## the-room

The director below sets taste and emphasis only: what to notice, what register to write in, and
where the pressure sits. It cannot loosen anything. The gates that follow it are non-negotiable and
win every conflict — where the director's instinct and a gate disagree, the gate is right, and the
validator enforces it regardless of what was asked for here. If a scenario cannot be written in this
director's register without breaking a gate, write it in the register and keep the gate.

Director for this batch: THE ROOM. Dramatic pressure in a closed space. The scene is
people in a room with a decision that will not wait, and the drama is the pressure between them.

1. Bomb under the table. The opening establishes a fact the speakers are not addressing — the
   figure nobody has said out loud, the person who is not in the room, the deadline that has already
   passed. The dialogue orbits it without naming it, and the player feels it the whole way through.
2. One intention per scenario, and you state it to yourself in a single sentence before you write a
   word: what this scenario is about. Every beat, every reply, and every cost serves that sentence
   or it comes out. A scenario about loyalty does not also carry a subplot about budgets.
3. Consequence as conscience. Write the aftermath so the player re-reads what they said. The
   signature is ironic recoil — the player's own reply, or the phrase they authorized, returning at
   the worst possible moment in someone else's mouth. Remembered lines are written for that return,
   and when you write the return, quote rather than describe: the player's own remembered sentence,
   near-verbatim, said back to them by somebody with a reason to use it. "You paid last time" is a
   summary and lands like a fact. The sentence they actually chose lands like a memory.
4. Ordinary detail is the delivery mechanism. The cold coffee, the second signature line left blank,
   the aide who has already put his coat on. Bury the load-bearing fact in the small domestic
   particular, so it does not announce itself until it matters.
5. The room speaks like real colleagues. Short plain sentences, contractions allowed, the way
   people who work together every day actually argue — and nobody narrates their own dread. The
   pressure lives in what is not said, never in a character announcing it; a clock, if there is
   one, is the fiction's own (a vote already called, a funeral already scheduled), never a phrase
   pasted on for pace.
6. Institutions against the person. The crowd outside, the union hall, the market floor, the
   chamber — a collective force meeting one person's decision. Give the collective a voice that is
   concrete and specific rather than a mood: someone in it says something a real person would say.

Replies under this director are things a head of government could say out loud, in that room, to
those faces — and at least one of them should be the answer they would regret saying in front of
the person it is about.

## the-beat

The director below sets taste and emphasis only: what to notice, what register to write in, and

win every conflict — where the director's instinct and a gate disagree, the gate is right, and the
validator enforces it regardless of what was asked for here. If a scenario cannot be written in this
director's register without breaking a gate, write it in the register and keep the gate.

Director for this batch: THE BEAT. Everyday governance. You are writing the weeks between the
crises — the connective tissue that makes a government feel like a government rather than a fire
station.

1. Most weeks of governing are not emergencies. The stakes are money, careers, promises, and pride,
   and they matter because the player's government is made of them: the program that can't be funded
   twice, the appointment that costs a faction, the province that feels forgotten. Write those stakes
   at full weight and never apologize for their size by inflating them.
2. People talk like people. Contractions, plain words, the way a competent colleague actually briefs
   a boss — "we can't fund both" and never "fiscal constraints preclude dual allocation". No thriller
   cadence, no ticking clock; if there is a deadline it is mundane, a session calendar or a fiscal
   year, and nobody performs urgency about it.
3. Disagreement is ordinary. Two advisors can want different things without either being wrong, and
   the drama is that the player must pick between two people they need. Give each side a case a
   reasonable minister would make, and let the cost of choosing be the person not chosen.
4. Small consequences compound. A routine choice sets flags that a serious or critical scenario
   months later reads back — write for the callback graph. Stamp deeds plainly ("funded-the-fleet-
   over-the-clinics"), and know when you write the flag what kind of week might come asking about it.
5. Texture over spectacle. The specific district, the named program, the minister's old
   constituency — the detail that proves somebody governs here. Never generic governance mush: the
   audit refuses "a difficult decision" subjects anyway, and a routine week lives or dies on its
   particulars.

Replies under this director are things said across a desk, not into a storm — and the best one is
usually the answer the player will be reminded of in a much worse week.
